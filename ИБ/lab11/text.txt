Later, as a young writer, I discovered that the best way to get inspired was to go to the Los Angeles library and wander around it, pulling books off the shelves, reading a line here, a paragraph there, snatching, devouring, moving on, and then suddenly writing on the first piece of paper I could find. I often stood for hours at card tables, scribbling on these scraps of paper (they were constantly kept in the library for research notes), afraid to stop and go home while I was possessed by this excitement.

Back then, I ate, drank, and slept with books of all kinds and sizes, colors, and countries.: This manifested itself later in the fact that when Hitler burned books, I felt it just as keenly as, forgive me, when he killed people, because for the entire long history of mankind they were one flesh. Whether it was my mind or my body, being thrown into a furnace was a sin, and I carried it inside me as I walked past countless fire station doors, patting service dogs, admiring my long reflection in the brass poles that firefighters use to slide down. And I often passed by fire stations, walking and returning from the library, day and night, in Illinois, as a boy.Among the notes about my life, I found many pages with descriptions of red cars and firefighters clattering with boots. And I remember one night when I heard a piercing scream from a room in my grandmother's house, I ran into that room, opened the door to look inside and screamed myself.

Because there, climbing the wall, was a glowing monster. He grew up before my eyes. It made a powerful roaring sound like it was coming from a furnace and seemed fantastically alive as it fed on the wallpaper and devoured the ceiling.

It was, of course, fire. But he seemed like a dazzling beast, and I'll never forget him and how he mesmerized me before we ran off to fill a bucket and kill him to death.

Perhaps these memories — of thousands of nights in a friendly, warm, huge darkness, with pools of green light from lamps, in libraries, and fire stations, and the evil fire that visited our house in person, combined later with the knowledge of new fireproof materials, served to make "Fahrenheit 451" grow out of the notes Into paragraphs, from paragraphs into a story:

Fahrenheit 451 was written entirely in the Los Angeles Library building, on a paid typewriter that I had to feed a dime to every half hour. I was writing in a room full of students who didn't know what I was doing there, just like I didn't know what they were doing there. Probably some other writer worked in this room. I like to think so. Is there a better place to work than the depths of a library?

But now I'm leaving, and I'm handing you over to myself, under the name Montag, in another year, with a nightmare, with a book clutched in my hand, and a book hidden in my head. Please take a short walk with him. It was a pleasure to burn. It's a special pleasure to see how fire consumes things, how they turn black and change. The copper tip of the hose is clenched in his fists, a huge python spews a poisonous stream of kerosene into the world, blood is pounding in his temples, and his hands seem like the hands of an outlandish conductor performing a symphony of fire and destruction, turning the torn, charred pages of history into ashes. The symbolic helmet, decorated with the number 451, is pulled low over his forehead, his eyes sparkle with orange flames at the thought of what is about to happen: he presses the igniter — and the fire greedily rushes at the house, coloring the evening sky in crimson-yellow-black tones. He is walking in a swarm of fiery red fireflies, and most of all he wants to do now what he so often amused himself with as a child - to stick a twig with a lollipop into the fire, while books, like pigeons, rustle with their wings, pages, dying on the porch and on the lawn in front of the house, they fly up in a fiery whirlwind, and the soot-blackened wind blows them away.

A hard smile froze on Montag's face, a smile like a grimace that appears on a man's lips when he is suddenly scorched by fire and he quickly recoils back from his hot touch.

He knew that when he returned to the fire station, he, the minstrel of fire, would look in the mirror and wink amiably at his burned, soot-smeared face. And later in the dark, already falling asleep, he will still feel a frozen, convulsive smile on his lips. She had never left his face, never since he could remember.

He carefully dried and hung up his shiny black helmet, carefully hung his canvas jacket next to it, washed himself with pleasure under a strong shower stream and, whistling, put his hands in his pockets, crossed the landing of the upper floor of the fire station and slipped through the hatch. At the last second, when disaster already seemed inevitable, he pulled his hands out of his pockets, wrapped them around a shiny bronze pole and skidded to a stop with a creak just before his feet touched the cement floor of the lower floor.

He walked out onto the deserted night street and headed for the subway. A silent pneumatic train swallowed him up, flew like a shuttle through a well-oiled tube of an underground tunnel and, together with a strong jet of warm air, threw him onto an escalator lined with yellow tiles leading to the surface in one of the suburbs.

Whistling, Montag rode up the escalator into the silence of the night. Without thinking about anything, at least nothing in particular, he reached the turn. But even before he reached the corner, he suddenly slowed down his steps, as if the wind had blown in from somewhere and hit him in the face or someone had called his name.

Several times already, approaching the turn in the evening, behind which the starlit sidewalk led to his house, he had experienced this strange feeling. It seemed to him that a moment before he turned, someone was standing around the corner. There was a special silence in the air, as if someone was hiding and waiting there, two steps away, and only a second before his appearance suddenly turned into a shadow and let him through.
