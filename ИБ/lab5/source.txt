It was a pleasure to burn. It's a special pleasure to see how fire consumes things, how they turn black and change. The copper tip of the hose is clenched in his fists, a huge python spews a poisonous stream of kerosene into the world, blood is pounding in his temples, and his hands seem like the hands of an outlandish conductor performing a symphony of fire and destruction, turning the torn, charred pages of history into ashes. The symbolic helmet, decorated with the number 451, is pulled low over his forehead, his eyes sparkle with orange flames at the thought of what is about to happen: he presses the igniter — and the fire greedily rushes at the house, coloring the evening sky in crimson-yellow-black tones. He walks in a swarm of fiery red fireflies, and most of all he wants to do now what he so often amused himself with as a child - to stick a twig with a lollipop into the fire, while books, like pigeons, rustle with their wings, pages, die on the porch and on the lawn in front of the house, they fly up in a fiery whirlwind, and the soot-blackened wind blows them away.

A hard smile froze on Montag's face, a smile like a grimace that appears on a man's lips when he is suddenly scorched by fire and he quickly recoils back from his hot touch.

He knew that when he returned to the fire station, he, the minstrel of fire, would look in the mirror and wink amiably at his burned, soot-smeared face. And later in the dark, already falling asleep, he will still feel a frozen, convulsive smile on his lips. She had never left his face, never since he could remember.

He carefully dried and hung up his shiny black helmet, carefully hung his canvas jacket next to it, washed himself with pleasure under a strong shower stream and, whistling, put his hands in his pockets, crossed the landing of the upper floor of the fire station and slipped through the hatch. At the last second, when disaster already seemed inevitable, he pulled his hands out of his pockets, wrapped them around a shiny bronze pole and skidded to a stop with a creak just before his feet touched the cement floor of the lower floor.

He walked out onto the deserted night street and headed for the subway. A silent pneumatic train swallowed him up, flew like a shuttle through a well-oiled tube of an underground tunnel and, together with a strong jet of warm air, threw him onto an escalator lined with yellow tiles leading to the surface in one of the suburbs.

Whistling, Montag rode up the escalator into the silence of the night. Without thinking about anything, at least nothing in particular, he reached the turn. But even before he reached the corner, he suddenly slowed down his steps, as if the wind had blown in from somewhere and hit him in the face or someone had called his name.

Several times already, approaching the turn in the evening, behind which the starlit sidewalk led to his house, he had experienced this strange feeling. It seemed to him that a moment before he turned, someone was standing around the corner. There was a special silence in the air, as if someone was hiding and waiting there, two steps away, and only a second before his appearance suddenly turned into a shadow and let him through.

Maybe his nostrils caught a faint scent, maybe he felt a slight increase in temperature on his face and hands near the place where someone invisible was standing, warming the air with his warmth. It was impossible to understand. However, every time he turned a corner, he saw only white slabs of a deserted sidewalk. Only once did he feel as if a shadow had flashed across the lawn, but it was gone before he could look closely or utter a single word.

Today, at the turn, he slowed down so much that he almost stopped. In his mind, he was already around the corner— and caught a faint rustle. Someone's breathing? Or the movement of the air caused by the presence of someone who was standing very quietly and waiting?

He turned the corner.

The wind blew autumn leaves along the moonlit sidewalk, and it seemed that the girl walking towards them was not stepping over the flagstones, but gliding over them, driven by the wind and foliage. She bent her head slightly and watched the toes of her shoes brush against the swirling leaves. Her delicate, matte-white face shone with gentle, insatiable curiosity. It expressed mild surprise. The dark eyes looked at the world so intently that it seemed nothing could escape them. She was wearing a white dress, it rustled. It seemed to Montag that he could hear every movement of her hands in time with her steps, that he could hear even that lightest, elusive sound— the bright flutter of her face when, raising her head, she suddenly saw that only a few steps separated her from the man standing in the middle of the sidewalk.
